Lyman Moore & Lyseth · 1999–2001

Downplayed the Risk

The material below is sourced from the Portland Press Herald archive. All the information is publicly available and verifiable. Articles involving specific children are not linked, but are available in the Press Herald archives. No information is taken from the Maine CDC cancer cluster investigation records described on the Investigation page. Each citation links to the original scanned notice or article.

This section continues directly from Part 1, picking up in April 1999. In a span of two and a half years, five children attending Lyseth and Lyman Moore were diagnosed with or died of cancer. School officials, a toxicologist, and an environmental consultant made a series of public statements about the safety of school buildings and the danger of asbestos and mold exposure.

1999

4/29/1999 - 9 year old Lyseth student dies of cancer.

6/12/1999 - Notice to contractors, Lyman Moore boiler replacement, asbestos removal required before replacement. The full notice is quoted in Part 1.

2000

2/5/2000 - Portland Public Schools' Asbestos Management Plan Annual Notification stated all facilities had been inspected for asbestos in compliance with the Asbestos Hazard Emergency Response Act (AHERA). Facilities noted as having asbestos included: PHS, DHS, all three middle schools, Adams, Baxter, Cliff Island, Hall, Jack, Longfellow, Lyseth, Peaks Island, Presumpscot, Reiche, Riverton, PATHS, Clifford, West, Multilingual Center, Central Office, Reed Warehouse, Maintenance Shop, and Exposition Building.

“The facilities are inspected bi-annually, in March and August, for any changes in conditions. Questions or concerns should be addressed to Hank Dresch, LEA Asbestos Coordinator at (telephone number).”

Portland Press Herald · 2/5/2000

4/18/2000 - 13 year old Lyman Moore student dies of cancer.

5/18/2000 - 11 year old Lyseth student diagnosed with cancer.

6/3/2000 - “Lewiston tops state list for renovations - Martel School will be closed again Monday because of asbestos.”

“An elementary school with high levels of asbestos was moved to the top of the list of schools awaiting renovations as experts sought to calm parents worried about the possible health effects. Superintendent Leon Levesque announced the emergency closure of Martel School on Wednesday. The next morning, teachers arrived at the school to find the doors locked.”

“Experts confirmed that Martel students were exposed to high levels of asbestos but sought to assure worried parents that health problems their children may have are not connected to the exposure. 'There really aren't any acute effects from this,' Dr. Susan Upsham, a Portland toxicologist, told 200 people at a meeting Thursday night in the Lewiston High School gymnasium.”

“Nonetheless, Levesque said clothing that students left at the school will not be released at this time. Porous items such as sweaters may have picked up asbestos fibers and should not be brought home anyway, said Mark Coleman of Environmental Safety & Hygiene Associates Inc., the Westbrook company that tested the school's air.”

“Coleman downplayed the risk of people bringing home asbestos fibers on other items. 'I think we need to get over the idea that we are going home with a lot of asbestos all over our clothing,' he said. But when a woman asked Coleman if he would get rid of a book bag if his child has used it all year at Martel, the consultant said, 'me, personally, yes.'”

“Asbestos, which has been used as pipe insulation, releases fibers that can cause cancer when inhaled. Coleman reported that tests showed asbestos levels higher than the federal government considers safe were found in some common areas of the school, including the gym.”

“Levesque said administrators are moving to fix the problems at the school. The Maine Department of Education has moved Martel's application for money for school renovations to the top of the list, he said.”

Portland Press Herald · 6/3/2000

The next day, 6/4/2000, “It was too short a life, but one filled with friendships and love” ran in the Press Herald. The article consists of children at Lyman Moore remembering their friend, who passed away from cancer in April.

8/14/2000 - 15 year old Lyman Moore student dies of cancer. The second in four months at the school.

8/31/2000 - “Playing fields can't support all the fun - A study concludes Portland needs new and improved fields or youth sports and recreation will suffer.”

“The effect of all this use has not been good for the fields. Many turn to dirt, or mud, once the season begins, and do not recover because of poor drainage and lack of irrigation.”

“Particularly chewed up are the fields at Fox Street, and Lyman Moore, Dougherty Field and Fitzpatrick Stadium.”

Portland Press Herald · 8/31/2000

10/19/2000 - “Portland gets final plan for schools - A task force studying the aging elementary schools recommends renovation and replacing, with no closings” ran in the Press Herald.

“A final proposal to upgrade Portland's aging elementary schools now carries a price tag of nearly $77 million, but basic tenets remain unchanged from last spring: Build some new schools, renovate others, but don't close any. The School Committee on Wednesday night formally received that recommendation from a city task force that has been studying the issue for more than a year.”

“The task force finalized its recommendation last June, but the project costs were reviewed over the summer and now are expected to be $76.8 million instead of $72.7 million, as estimated then.”

“Crowded Lyseth Elementary School on Auburn Street would not only be renovated but a new school to house half of its more than 670 students would be built somewhere in North Deering.”

“The task force's final report anticipates that the replacement schools would be completed in 2006 and renovations on the other schools in 2007. But Banks said those dates are estimates and that it could take longer. The goal, he said, is 'steady progress year after year.'”

Portland Press Herald · 10/19/2000

2001

On the same Portland Press Herald Section B cover page, two stories about carcinogens affecting children in Maine are published:

3/1/2001 - “Lead threat can elude simple home tests - Officials say parents should learn how to eliminate their children's exposure to lead,”

“Susan Thornfeldt, director of the Maine Lead Action Project, hopes that parents will not ignore the serious message that statistic sends. They need to check their homes to see if they are lead-safe. 'Whatever happens to (children who are lead poisoned), whether it be learning disabilities or behavioral problems or reduced IQ, that's something that can never be fixed,' she said. 'It's permanent and lifelong.'”

Portland Press Herald · 3/1/2001

3/1/2001 - “Schools' air safe, parents assured - A second round of tests shows no asbestos contamination at Park Street School. A public forum is set tonight,”

“Kennebunk - Town and state officials say they believe the air at Park Street School is safe, despite fears by some parents that there may be an asbestos problem.”

“A contractor removed asbestos tiles during a carpet-installation job in two classrooms during last week's winter vacation.”

“Carole Cifrino, the state's coordinator for asbestos-hazard prevention, also said the job was done correctly. 'No asbestos was remaining,' Cifrino said.”

“Superintendent Barbara Pillsbury closed the two rooms until Wednesday when results from a second round of tests showed no contamination.”

“This is the second time asbestos problems have confronted local school officials. Last fall, Cousens School was closed for several months after a heavy rainstorm flooded the school during a roof project, exposing asbestos ceiling and floor tiles. School officials delayed reopening Cousens until November after parents said they were concerned about their children's safety.”

“Asbestos, which has been used for years as pipe insulation and in floor and ceiling tiles, releases fibers that can cause cancer when inhaled.”

“The asbestos issue at Park Street surfaced as part of the school district's effort to remove mold from the building. The mold was caused by moisture that built up over the years due to roof leaks. It may have made some students with allergies more prone to illness, school officials said.”

Portland Press Herald · 3/1/2001

3/2/2001 - “Pupils' parents reassured about asbestos - Tests show no fibers escaped at a Kennebunk school, despite 'technical' removal violations”

“Kennebunk - The contractors who removed asbestos from a local school committed 'technical violations' of regulations governing such projects, state environmental officials said Thursday night. But officials also said that the violations involved only work procedures - and that tests showed no asbestos escaped into the air during the project. They said they still need to determine what action, if any, to take against the contractors.”

“John Bucci and Carole Cifrino of the Maine Department of Environmental Protection told parents Thursday night they are investigating to determine the extent of contractor violations during the removal of some broken tiles. 'There were some technical violations,' Bucci said. Cifrino said the contractors failed to put up enough barriers to prevent the escape of asbestos dust.”

“Cifrino said the recent air tests confirmed that, despite the procedural violations, no contamination occurred.”

“Christina Holbrook, another parent, said she feels reassured that the school is safe for her son, who is in the third grade. But she said she will be interested to see what the state does after completing its investigation of the procedures followed during the project.”

Portland Press Herald · 3/2/2001

8/16/2001 - “Discovery of mold forces elementary school to close - Officials scramble to find classrooms for 300 Portland pupils who would have attended next month” ran in the Press Herald.

“Three weeks before the start of the new school year in Portland, officials decided Wednesday to permanently close Jack Elementary School because they believe it poses a health risk to students and staff.”

“Mary Jo O'Connor, Portland's superintendent of schools, told School Committee members that she learned late Tuesday that a mold called stachybotrys, which can aggravate asthma and otherwise make people sick, is airborne at the school.”

“'Throughout the summer,' she said, 'we have been reassured with each successive test that the levels of mold and mildew were well within the normal range... That changed yesterday afternoon very late in the day.' O'Connor said she does not believe the mold can be removed, and that, to keep students and staff members safe she wants to close the school.”

“School Committee members concurred, although they did not vote. They didn't have to and, in fact, are not permitted to vote during workshop sessions. The decision was O'Connor's, though the board members supported it.”

“The 59-year-old school…has long had air quality problems, she said. That's why several consultants tested it this summer. They found stachybotrys contamination in four classrooms, but in walls or under ceiling panels, where it is not dangerous. 'It is very much like asbestos,' O'Connor said. 'If you don't touch it, it's not going to bother anyone.' Then they found it in the air, and started to worry. 'These things multiply and divide and spread,' O'Connor said.”

“Because she could not guarantee that the school was safe, she decided to shut it. Jill Duson, who chairs the School Committee, said she understands many people will be concerned - not only Jack parents, students and staff members, but people affiliated with other elementary schools. 'We're going to work very quickly to come up with a plan to make sure teaching and learning still happen,' she said. 'We're costing out all the options.'”

Portland Press Herald · 8/16/2001

9/8/2001 - A kindergartener at Lyseth is diagnosed with cancer. The 5th student to have cancer at Lyseth and Lyman Moore in under 2.5 years.
