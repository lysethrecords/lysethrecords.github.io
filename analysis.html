Combining the results: How unlikely is this pattern?

Testing each cancer in isolation ignores that they are all occurring in the same neighborhood, in the same population, at the same time. Fisher's method is a published technique for combining separate probabilities into one, answering: What are the odds of seeing this whole pattern together by chance?

The result

Combining the overall cancer rate for children (0–19) with the overall rate for young adults (20–44) against the Maine baseline:

p = 0.000075

Roughly 1 in 13,389 that the combined elevation across both young age groups arose by chance alone. Inputs: 0–19, 10 cases vs 2.1 expected (p=0.0001); 20–44, 53 vs 39.2 expected (p=0.0412).

A note on method (independence). Fisher's method assumes the combined tests are independent. These are not fully independent as North Deering's rates all come from the same population. Testing each cancer separately as the state's report did, treats the cancers as independent events. A permutation test, redistributing cases at random to see how often chance produces a pattern this concentrated, would confirm the result without any independence assumption.
